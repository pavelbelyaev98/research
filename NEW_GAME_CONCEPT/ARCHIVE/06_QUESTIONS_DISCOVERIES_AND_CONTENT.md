# 06 — Questions, Round 4: Discoveries and Content

Rules, categories and variations — exact counts and prices are prototype-tuned (Rule 8). Answer by ID
in chat; answers go to `DECISIONS.md`.

This round defines what the underground actually contains: the find categories, how the detector
chooses targets, how value and rarity work, how clusters are built, and what the first five authored
objects should be.

---

### D01 — Common finds roster
**Context:** Common finds are the reliable income, always detector-silent, recognized by shape when
excavated.
**Options:**
- **A)** 20–30 types: bottles, plain stones, the mineral ladder (coal → copper → iron → silver → gold →
  emerald → ruby → diamond), commonplace scrap, packaging and rubbish. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** 10–15 types; leaner, more repetition.
- **C)** 40+ types; richer, heavier asset load for a 3–5 h game.
**Recommendation:** A. Common means *routine to find repeatedly*, and 20–30 gives each zone enough
texture without turning production into a content treadmill. `[OMT]`'s huge junk roster was its
identity; here it is the baseline.
> **ANSWER:**

### D02 — Distinctive finds roster
**Context:** These are the memorable discoveries: appliances, homemade machines, old tools/weapons,
fossils, strange mechanical objects, increasingly impossible things.
**Options:**
- **A)** 30–50 types, produced in reviewed batches (five-object first slice before bulk). *(Recommended)*
- **B)** 15–20 types, higher quality per object but fewer surprises.
- **C)** 60+ types; risks quality and scope.
**Recommendation:** A. `[SPOT]`'s handcrafted underground was its best-loved asset; `[OMT]`'s
"deeper you go, weirder it gets" was its hook. Density of *recognizable* objects is the content here,
so author fewer categories but many silhouettes.
> **ANSWER:**

### D03 — What each find category is for
**Context:** We need clean tiers so money, the display and the mystery each get fed. W08b confirmed
uniques are kept on display.
**Options:**
- **A)** Two tiers: ordinary (sell) and unique (display + perk, unsellable). Simplest, but income
  becomes unstable if many finds are "unique".
- **B)** Three tiers: **common** (sell), **distinctive** (first find goes to the display, duplicates
  sell), **unique** (one-off, display + passive effect). *(Recommended)*
- **C)** Player chooses keep-or-sell on every non-common first find.
**Recommendation:** B. It grows the display the way you want, keeps duplicates as income, and keeps
uniques special. It also gives the detector three meaningful target classes without revealing value.
> **ANSWER:**

### D04 — Detector eligibility
**Context:** The detector is the curiosity engine; it must not become a value radar.
**Options:**
- **A)** Signals only noteworthy categories (distinctive and unique); never commons; not every
  distinctive signals (physical eligibility authored per object); size, metal content and price never
  decide eligibility. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Signals every non-common find, always.
- **C)** Signals by value threshold (anything above X money).
**Recommendation:** A. C would turn the game into a treasure GPS and make ordinary finds feel like
waste; B would make cues constant and routine. A few silent distinctive finds are essential: they
keep digging itself rewarding, not just signal-chasing.
> **ANSWER:**

### D05 — Value and rarity bands
**Context:** Prices should stay legible and never break the economy with jackpots.
**Options:**
- **A)** Four bands: common income, distinctive value, rare (several expeditions), unique (permanent
  effect instead of money). Rare finds never buy half the tree. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Three bands.
- **C)** Continuous value spectrum (prices float by depth/size).
**Recommendation:** A. Band values are readable to the player and tunable by the designer; the rare
band exists mainly to create "I can finally afford that upgrade" moments.
> **ANSWER:**

### D06 — Related-object clusters
**Context:** Clusters are the main driver of lateral digging: a bone suggests more bones, a wheel
suggests a vehicle, fragments suggest a machine.
**Options:**
- **A)** Five micro-scene templates to start (bone scatter, vehicle parts, household cluster, machine
  fragments, odd ritual arrangement), rotated and placed procedurally; relationships authored, no
  pre-dug chambers. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Independent placement only.
- **C)** Ten+ templates now.
**Recommendation:** A. It is the cheapest way to make the underground feel authored, and it directly
manufactures the "I wonder if the rest is nearby" moment that drives the loop.
> **ANSWER:**

### D07 — First vertical-slice objects
**Context:** Before any bulk production, five recognizable objects prove the reveal-and-recognize
loop. They should be quickly identifiable from partial shapes and span scales.
**Options:**
- **A)** One appliance, one tool, one machine part, one fossil, one household oddity (e.g., washing
  machine, hand drill, gearbox, mammoth bone, gramophone). *(Recommended)*
- **B)** All five from one category (e.g., five appliances) to test variety within a family.
- **C)** The team decides later.
**Recommendation:** A. The slice must prove that *recognition* works across silhouettes; testing five
of the same shape teaches less.
> **ANSWER:**

### D08 — Mystery escalation props
**Context:** The mystery is built from objects that increasingly should not be there. The final
discovery is a modern object built in impossibly ancient materials.
**Options:**
- **A)** Three escalation steps: (1) anachronistic junk (a modern object too deep), (2) objects that
  are *too correct* (pristine, deliberate), (3) clearly constructed impossibilities. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** One hard swerve near the end.
- **C)** Scattered oddities with no structure.
**Recommendation:** A. `[AGADH]`'s ending was called a bait-and-switch because nothing prepared it;
a three-step trail makes the finale feel earned rather than random.
> **ANSWER:**

### D09 — Special key/component objects
**Context:** A small number of objects outside the inventory drive the final sequence.
**Options:**
- **A)** Three or four components; never lost; simple obvious use; no puzzle. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** None; the final object is simply found.
- **C)** Many components with a combination puzzle.
**Recommendation:** A. Enough to make the ending feel assembled by the player without smuggling in a
puzzle game (NOT-list).
> **ANSWER:**

### D10 — Large discoveries
**Context:** Occasional scale changes keep early and late play visually different, but large objects
are not the focus.
**Options:**
- **A)** A few per run (e.g., 3–5): a car, a large appliance pile, a section of machinery; extracted
  by cable-yank; some can stay in place. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Many large objects; risks becoming a vehicle salvage game.
- **C)** None; handheld and appliance scale only.
**Recommendation:** A. `[OMT]`'s cars/spaceship were memorable highlights; keeping them rare makes
them events instead of chores.
> **ANSWER:**

### D11 — Display wall details
**Context:** Uniques and first finds are kept physically visible at the surface.
**Options:**
- **A)** A growing wall/shelf: uniques and first-find distinctives on display with name + depth;
  expandable; duplicates sell. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Fixed slots; the player must choose what to display.
- **C)** Every find displayed (hundreds of items); turns the wall into clutter.
**Recommendation:** A. It delivers the "always visible" desire while keeping the wall readable and
growing as a personal record.
> **ANSWER:**

### D12 — Content pacing rules
**Context:** Generation validates placement so every run opens well and never dumps novelty.
**Options:**
- **A)** Guaranteed noteworthy find early; bounded dry spells; related objects clustered; no more than
  one major-scale discovery per band; late bands keep new silhouettes appearing. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Fully free distribution.
- **C)** Fixed order of discoveries.
**Recommendation:** A. `[AGADH]`'s late game was called empty; `[OMT]`'s back half was called
repetitive. These rules are the direct antidote.
> **ANSWER:**

### D13 — Cultural references
**Context:** Junk can carry jokes and references (like `[OMT]`'s famous game cartridge), but dated
references age badly and can create legal risk.
**Options:**
- **A)** Original-parody references only: objects evoke a genre/era without naming real brands or
  games. *(Recommended)*
- **B)** Direct pop-culture references.
- **C)** No references; pure period-neutral junk.
**Recommendation:** A. It keeps the humor without turning the game into a museum of 2020s memes, and
it avoids the "asset-flip/legal" risk profile entirely.
> **ANSWER:**

## Expanded explanations (added 2026-09-14 on request)

### D06 — Related-object clusters, in practice
Instead of placing every find independently, the generator sometimes buries a small authored *scene*
of related objects as a group. The player hits one, and the rest suggests itself. Five starting
templates:
1. **Bone scatter** — skull fragment, ribs, tusk piece spread over a few meters.
2. **Vehicle parts** — wheel, axle, bumper, engine block, arranged as if a car sank here.
3. **Household cluster** — plates, bottles, a stove, a sewing machine in a collapsed heap.
4. **Machine fragments** — gears, drive shaft, boiler plate leading toward something larger.
5. **Odd arrangement** — deliberately placed objects (a circle of bottles around a tool), which also
   feeds the mystery.
Rules: authored relationships, rotated and placed with slight jitter, validated for spacing, counted
once in the finite population, never requiring a pre-dug chamber.

### D07 — First five vertical-slice objects, and what each tests
Before mass-producing 30–50 finds, five objects prove the recognition loop: can a player tell what an
object is from a partial silhouette, and do they voluntarily keep uncovering it?
1. **Washing machine** — tests box + circular door readability at large appliance scale.
2. **Hand drill** — tests small handheld silhouette recognition.
3. **Gearbox / engine block** — tests machine-part recognition and naturally leads into a cluster.
4. **Mammoth bone / tusk** — tests organic-curve recognition; ties to the Danube bones inspiration.
5. **Gramophone** — tests a funny, display-worthy oddity with a distinctive horn shape.
Metrics for the slice: time to recognize, percentage who fully uncover, recall after the session,
and the reaction when identity lands.

### D08 — Mystery escalation, step by step
1. **Anachronistic junk** (zone 2–3): objects too modern for their depth — a soda can below old
   sediment, a rubber duck in an ancient layer. Dismissible as a joke at first.
2. **Too correct** (zone 3–4): a rustless tool, a bottle standing upright under tons of sediment, a
   manufactured part matching no nearby machine. "Something put this here."
3. **Constructed impossibilities** (zone 4): machinery built from ancient materials with a modern
   function; ends at the final object (a modern object built impossibly long ago, Q12).
Never signposted by UI or NPCs; the display wall lets the player assemble the story themselves.

### D09 — Ending components, in practice
3–4 objects outside the bag that lead to the finale: parts of the final mechanism (e.g., three
segments fitting a housing found at the bottom). They never take inventory slots, are never lost in
recovery, live in a small permanent case/display, and insert with one obvious action at the final
object. No combination puzzle. Their job: the ending is assembled by your digging, and components can
be placed off the main shaft to reward lateral exploration.
